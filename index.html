Research

Resistive switching in SiOx (B.Sc. thesis, BME, 2024 to 2026)

My thesis is about how a thin film of silicon oxide switches its resistance when you push current through it. There are two competing explanations. In one, the conducting path forms from oxygen vacancies percolating through the oxide. The other is a more filament-like picture. I'm trying to find out which one reproduces the resistance noise measured in the lab.

The simulation is a stack. LAMMPS with a machine-learned potential makes the glass, a resistor network sits on its vacancy sites, and on top of that a kinetic Monte Carlo model grows filaments with Joule heating.

It's early. One result: for one choice of barrier parameters I ran 30 seeds at each of 7 bias levels. Filaments grew at every bias but only survived at low bias. All 30 runs were stable at 0.5 to 1.0 V, and none were at 2.5 V or above. Against the measured noise the model is still off, by roughly as much as the static fit it starts from (R² of 0.53).

Generative LEGO (2025, restarted 2026)

The goal is text or a photo in, a LEGO model you can build out. It's based on BrickNet (CVPR 2026). I first tried this in 2025 and restarted it this year, with LLM captions and lately diffusion-based generation.

It doesn't work well yet. The best setup is still the stock BrickNet checkpoint with 8 samples per prompt, a judge to pick the best one, and a check for collisions and loose bricks. I once reported a fine-tuned model as better, then re-checked and retracted it. Nothing tests yet whether a result would stand up under gravity. The details are in the research log.

Lilith (BadCompany, CTO, 2025 to 2026)

At BadCompany I was CTO and we built Lilith, a security product for AI agents. The trouble with agents that call tools is that someone can talk them into things you never wanted, and a check inside the agent doesn't help much, because the agent is what's being attacked. So we put the rules outside it. Each tool call was checked against a Cedar policy, and on Linux the kernel enforced the same policy, so even a compromised agent couldn't open files or reach hosts it wasn't allowed to. We delivered it over 2025 and 2026 and wrote it up on badcompany.xyz. It's finished.

gpu-waterz (CUDA)

waterz turns a network's predicted affinities into labelled cells for connectomics. This is a GPU version of it. I wanted three things: big volumes that fit on a 24 GB 3090, speed, and a CPU alternative. On a 5090 it handles a 2.16 billion voxel volume in 3.1 seconds, with segmentation quality (VOI) within 0.02 of stock waterz. The merging is a parallel approximation of the sequential algorithm.

The hard part was getting the same answer twice. Regions get merged by the mean affinity along their shared boundary, and I was summing those with float atomic adds. Float addition isn't associative and the GPU adds in whatever order threads arrive, so two identical runs disagreed on about 740,000 of 7.5 million edges. That flips merge decisions and changes the labels. Switching to fixed-point integer accumulation fixed it. Integer addition doesn't care about order, so the output is now byte-identical on every run.
