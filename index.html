Research

Resistive switching in SiOx (B.Sc. thesis, BME, 2024 to 2026)

My thesis is on how a thin film of silicon oxide switches its resistance when current goes through it. I'm comparing two explanations against the noise we measure in the lab. In one, the conducting path forms from oxygen vacancies percolating through the oxide. In the other it's a more filament-like picture.

To simulate it I use LAMMPS with a machine-learned potential to make the glass, put a resistor network on its vacancy sites, and run a kinetic Monte Carlo model on top that grows filaments with Joule heating.

It's early. For one choice of barrier parameters I ran 30 seeds at each of 7 bias levels. Filaments grew at every bias, but they only survived at low bias: all 30 runs were stable at 0.5 to 1.0 V and none were at 2.5 V or above. The noise is still off compared to the lab data, about as much as in the simpler static fit I started from (R² of 0.53).

Generative LEGO (2025, restarted 2026)

I want to give it a sentence or a photo and get back a LEGO model I can build. It's based on BrickNet (CVPR 2026). I tried this in 2025 and started again this year, with LLM captions and lately diffusion-based generation.

It doesn't work well yet. The best setup is still stock BrickNet with 8 samples per prompt, a judge to pick the best one, and a check for collisions and loose bricks. I once reported a fine-tuned model as better, then re-checked it and took the claim back. And nothing checks yet whether a result would stand up under gravity.

Lilith (BadCompany, 2025 to 2026)

At BadCompany I was responsible for technology, and we built Lilith, a security product for AI agents. If an agent can call tools, someone can talk it into doing things you never wanted. Checking inside the agent doesn't help much, because the agent is what's under attack. So the rules sit outside it. Every tool call was checked against a Cedar policy, and on Linux the kernel enforced the same policy, so even a compromised agent couldn't open files or reach hosts it wasn't allowed to. We delivered it, and the project is finished now.

gpu-waterz (CUDA)

waterz turns the affinities a neural network predicts into labelled cells, for connectomics. This is a GPU version. I wanted it to fit big volumes on a 24 GB 3090, to be fast, and to have a CPU alternative too. On a 5090 it does a 2.16 billion voxel volume in 3.1 seconds, and the segmentation quality (VOI) is within 0.02 of stock waterz. The merging is a parallel approximation of the sequential algorithm, not an exact port.

The hardest part was getting the same result twice. Regions get merged by the mean affinity along their shared boundary, and I was summing those with float atomic adds. Float addition isn't associative and the GPU adds in whatever order threads arrive, so two identical runs disagreed on about 740,000 of 7.5 million edges. That flips merge decisions, which changes the labels. Fixed-point integer accumulation fixed it, because integer addition doesn't care about order. Now the output is byte-identical on every run.

lean-interval-bounds (Lean 4)

Lean 4 tactics that prove bounds on exp, log, sin and cos in Mathlib. There's an approx tactic and a norm_num extension. The Lean kernel checks every proof, so you don't have to trust any floating point.
